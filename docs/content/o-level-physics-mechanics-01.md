## Q1 · difficulty 1

**Stem**

A runner covers $400\,\text{m}$ in $50\,\text{s}$. What is the runner's average speed?

**Options**

- A. $0.125\,\text{m s}^{-1}$
- B. $8\,\text{m s}^{-1}$
- C. $20\,000\,\text{m s}^{-1}$
- D. $450\,\text{m s}^{-1}$

**Correct:** B

**Solution**

$$
\text{average speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} = \frac{400\,\text{m}}{50\,\text{s}} = 8\,\text{m s}^{-1}
$$

**Answer: B**

Option A divides time by distance. Option C multiplies them. A quick check: a fast human runs about $10\,\text{m s}^{-1}$, so only B is realistic.

## Q2 · difficulty 1

**Stem**

A car speeds up uniformly from $5\,\text{m s}^{-1}$ to $25\,\text{m s}^{-1}$ in $4\,\text{s}$. What is its acceleration?

**Options**

- A. $5\,\text{m s}^{-2}$
- B. $6.25\,\text{m s}^{-2}$
- C. $7.5\,\text{m s}^{-2}$
- D. $80\,\text{m s}^{-2}$

**Correct:** A

**Solution**

Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time taken.

$$
a = \frac{v - u}{t} = \frac{25 - 5}{4} = \frac{20}{4} = 5\,\text{m s}^{-2}
$$

**Answer: A**

Option B uses the final velocity only ($25 \div 4$) and forgets the car was already moving. Option C adds the velocities instead of subtracting.

## Q3 · difficulty 1

**Stem**

On a velocity–time graph, what does the **area under the graph** represent?

**Options**

- A. Acceleration
- B. Speed
- C. Distance travelled
- D. Force

**Correct:** C

**Solution**

Area under a velocity–time graph = velocity × time = distance travelled (displacement, if direction matters).

The **gradient** of a velocity–time graph gives the acceleration. **Answer: C**

Option A confuses the area with the gradient, the most common mistake with these graphs.

## Q4 · difficulty 2

**Stem**

A car starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at $2\,\text{m s}^{-2}$ for $6\,\text{s}$. How far does it travel in this time?

**Options**

- A. $12\,\text{m}$
- B. $72\,\text{m}$
- C. $24\,\text{m}$
- D. $36\,\text{m}$

**Correct:** D

**Solution**

Use $s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ with $u = 0$ (from rest).

$$
s = 0 + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 6^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 36 = 36\,\text{m}
$$

**Answer: D**

Option A is $at$, which is the final **velocity** ($12\,\text{m s}^{-1}$), not the distance. Option B forgets the $\tfrac{1}{2}$.

## Q5 · difficulty 1

**Stem**

A stone is dropped from rest and falls freely for $3\,\text{s}$. Ignoring air resistance, what is its speed after $3\,\text{s}$? (Take $g = 10\,\text{m s}^{-2}$.)

**Options**

- A. $45\,\text{m s}^{-1}$
- B. $30\,\text{m s}^{-1}$
- C. $3.3\,\text{m s}^{-1}$
- D. $90\,\text{m s}^{-1}$

**Correct:** B

**Solution**

Use $v = u + at$ with $u = 0$ and $a = g = 10\,\text{m s}^{-2}$.

$$
v = 0 + 10 \times 3 = 30\,\text{m s}^{-1}
$$

**Answer: B**

Option A is the **distance** fallen, $\tfrac{1}{2}gt^2 = 45\,\text{m}$, not the speed. Option D uses $gt^2$.

## Q6 · difficulty 1

**Stem**

A resultant force of $20\,\text{N}$ acts on a body of mass $4\,\text{kg}$. What is the acceleration of the body?

**Options**

- A. $80\,\text{m s}^{-2}$
- B. $0.2\,\text{m s}^{-2}$
- C. $5\,\text{m s}^{-2}$
- D. $24\,\text{m s}^{-2}$

**Correct:** C

**Solution**

Newton's second law: $F = ma$, so

$$
a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{20\,\text{N}}{4\,\text{kg}} = 5\,\text{m s}^{-2}
$$

**Answer: C**

Option A multiplies instead of dividing. Option B divides the mass by the force.

## Q7 · difficulty 2

**Stem**

An astronaut has a mass of $60\,\text{kg}$ on Earth. What is the astronaut's mass on the Moon, where the gravitational field strength is $1.6\,\text{N kg}^{-1}$?

**Options**

- A. $60\,\text{kg}$
- B. $96\,\text{kg}$
- C. $37.5\,\text{kg}$
- D. $600\,\text{kg}$

**Correct:** A

**Solution**

**Mass** is the amount of matter in a body. It does not depend on where the body is, so it is still $60\,\text{kg}$ on the Moon.

What changes is the **weight**: on the Moon $W = mg = 60 \times 1.6 = 96\,\text{N}$. **Answer: A**

Option B is the Moon weight written with the wrong unit. Option D is the Earth weight ($600\,\text{N}$), again with the wrong unit.

## Q8 · difficulty 1

**Stem**

When a moving bus brakes suddenly, the standing passengers lurch forward. Why?

**Options**

- A. Friction pushes them forward.
- B. The floor of the bus pushes them forward.
- C. Gravity pulls them forward.
- D. Their bodies tend to keep moving forward because of inertia.

**Correct:** D

**Solution**

By Newton's first law, a body keeps moving at the same velocity unless a resultant force acts on it. This tendency to resist changes in motion is **inertia**.

The brakes stop the bus, but the passengers' bodies were moving forward and tend to keep moving. They appear to be thrown forward, but no forward force is acting on them. **Answer: D**

## Q9 · difficulty 3

**Stem**

A book rests on a table. The Earth pulls the book downward with a force (its weight). According to Newton's third law, which force forms a pair with this weight?

**Options**

- A. The table pushing up on the book
- B. The book pulling the Earth upward
- C. The book pushing down on the table
- D. Friction between the book and the table

**Correct:** B

**Solution**

A Newton's third law pair acts on **two different bodies** and is the **same type** of force. Here the weight is the Earth pulling the book (gravitational), so its partner is the book pulling the Earth upward (gravitational) with an equal force. **Answer: B**

Option A is the most tempting. The table's push is equal and opposite to the weight, but both forces act on the **same** body (the book) and are of different types, so they are not a third-law pair. They balance because the book is in equilibrium.

## Q10 · difficulty 1

**Stem**

A car of mass $1500\,\text{kg}$ moves at $20\,\text{m s}^{-1}$. What is its momentum?

**Options**

- A. $30\,000\,\text{kg m s}^{-1}$
- B. $75\,\text{kg m s}^{-1}$
- C. $300\,000\,\text{kg m s}^{-1}$
- D. $1520\,\text{kg m s}^{-1}$

**Correct:** A

**Solution**

$$
p = mv = 1500 \times 20 = 30\,000\,\text{kg m s}^{-1}
$$

**Answer: A**

Option C is the **kinetic energy** $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = 300\,000\,\text{J}$, a different quantity. Option B divides mass by velocity.

## Q11 · difficulty 3

**Stem**

A trolley of mass $2\,\text{kg}$ moving at $6\,\text{m s}^{-1}$ collides with a stationary trolley of mass $1\,\text{kg}$. They stick together. What is their common velocity just after the collision?

**Options**

- A. $6\,\text{m s}^{-1}$
- B. $3\,\text{m s}^{-1}$
- C. $4\,\text{m s}^{-1}$
- D. $12\,\text{m s}^{-1}$

**Correct:** C

**Solution**

Momentum is conserved: total momentum before = total momentum after.

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2 \times 6) + (1 \times 0) &= (2 + 1)\,v \\
12 &= 3v \\
v &= 4\,\text{m s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Answer: C**

Option B shares the speed between two trolleys and ignores their masses. Option A forgets that the moving mass has increased. Option D is the total momentum, not the velocity.

## Q12 · difficulty 1

**Stem**

A force of $50\,\text{N}$ pushes a box $4\,\text{m}$ along the floor in the direction of the force. How much work is done?

**Options**

- A. $12.5\,\text{J}$
- B. $54\,\text{J}$
- C. $0.08\,\text{J}$
- D. $200\,\text{J}$

**Correct:** D

**Solution**

Work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force.

$$
W = Fd = 50 \times 4 = 200\,\text{J}
$$

**Answer: D**

Option A divides instead of multiplying. Option B adds the numbers.

## Q13 · difficulty 2

**Stem**

A student of mass $50\,\text{kg}$ runs up a staircase of vertical height $6\,\text{m}$ in $10\,\text{s}$. What useful power does the student develop? (Take $g = 10\,\text{N kg}^{-1}$.)

**Options**

- A. $30\,\text{W}$
- B. $300\,\text{W}$
- C. $3000\,\text{W}$
- D. $30\,000\,\text{W}$

**Correct:** B

**Solution**

The work done is the gain in potential energy, $mgh$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
W &= mgh = 50 \times 10 \times 6 = 3000\,\text{J} \\
P &= \frac{W}{t} = \frac{3000}{10} = 300\,\text{W}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Answer: B**

Option C is the energy (work), not the power: it forgets to divide by the time. Option A forgets $g$, using mass instead of weight.

## Q14 · difficulty 1

**Stem**

What is the kinetic energy of a $2\,\text{kg}$ ball moving at $3\,\text{m s}^{-1}$?

**Options**

- A. $9\,\text{J}$
- B. $6\,\text{J}$
- C. $18\,\text{J}$
- D. $3\,\text{J}$

**Correct:** A

**Solution**

$$
E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 3^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 9 = 9\,\text{J}
$$

**Answer: A**

Option B is $mv$, the momentum. Option C forgets the $\tfrac{1}{2}$. Option D forgets to square the velocity.

## Q15 · difficulty 3

**Stem**

A ball is dropped from rest from a height of $5\,\text{m}$. Ignoring air resistance, what is its speed just before it hits the ground? (Take $g = 10\,\text{m s}^{-2}$.)

**Options**

- A. $50\,\text{m s}^{-1}$
- B. $100\,\text{m s}^{-1}$
- C. $10\,\text{m s}^{-1}$
- D. $7.1\,\text{m s}^{-1}$

**Correct:** C

**Solution**

The potential energy lost becomes kinetic energy.

$$
\begin{aligned}
mgh &= \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \\
v^2 &= 2gh = 2 \times 10 \times 5 = 100 \\
v &= 10\,\text{m s}^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The mass cancels, so it is not needed. **Answer: C**

Option B forgets to take the square root. Option D forgets the 2: $\sqrt{gh} = \sqrt{50} \approx 7.1$.

## Q16 · difficulty 2

**Stem**

A uniform metre rule is balanced on a pivot at its $50\,\text{cm}$ mark. A $2\,\text{N}$ weight hangs at the $20\,\text{cm}$ mark. Where must a $3\,\text{N}$ weight hang for the rule to balance?

**Options**

- A. At the $30\,\text{cm}$ mark
- B. At the $80\,\text{cm}$ mark
- C. At the $70\,\text{cm}$ mark
- D. At the $60\,\text{cm}$ mark

**Correct:** C

**Solution**

The rule is uniform and pivoted at its centre, so its own weight has no moment. By the principle of moments, the clockwise moment equals the anticlockwise moment.

The $2\,\text{N}$ weight is $50 - 20 = 30\,\text{cm}$ from the pivot:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2 \times 30 &= 3 \times d \\
d &= 20\,\text{cm}
\end{aligned}
$$

The $3\,\text{N}$ weight must be $20\,\text{cm}$ on the **other** side of the pivot: $50 + 20 = 70\,\text{cm}$ mark. **Answer: C**

Option B puts the weight at the same distance (30 cm), which only works for equal weights. Option A is on the same side as the $2\,\text{N}$ weight and would tip the rule further.

## Q17 · difficulty 2

**Stem**

A box of weight $600\,\text{N}$ rests on the ground on a rectangular face measuring $2\,\text{m}$ by $1.5\,\text{m}$. What pressure does it exert on the ground?

**Options**

- A. $1800\,\text{Pa}$
- B. $300\,\text{Pa}$
- C. $0.005\,\text{Pa}$
- D. $200\,\text{Pa}$

**Correct:** D

**Solution**

Area of the face: $2 \times 1.5 = 3\,\text{m}^2$.

$$
p = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{600\,\text{N}}{3\,\text{m}^2} = 200\,\text{Pa}
$$

**Answer: D**

Option B divides by one side only ($600 \div 2$) instead of the area. Option A multiplies force by area.

## Q18 · difficulty 2

**Stem**

What is the pressure due to water at a depth of $5\,\text{m}$? (Density of water $= 1000\,\text{kg m}^{-3}$, $g = 10\,\text{N kg}^{-1}$.)

**Options**

- A. $5000\,\text{Pa}$
- B. $50\,000\,\text{Pa}$
- C. $50\,\text{Pa}$
- D. $500\,000\,\text{Pa}$

**Correct:** B

**Solution**

Pressure in a liquid: $p = h\rho g$.

$$
p = 5 \times 1000 \times 10 = 50\,000\,\text{Pa}
$$

**Answer: B**

Option A forgets $g$. Option C forgets the density.

## Q19 · difficulty 2

**Stem**

A spring stretches by $2\,\text{cm}$ when a load of $4\,\text{N}$ hangs from it. What is its extension when the load is $10\,\text{N}$? (The limit of proportionality is not exceeded.)

**Options**

- A. $5\,\text{cm}$
- B. $20\,\text{cm}$
- C. $8\,\text{cm}$
- D. $0.8\,\text{cm}$

**Correct:** A

**Solution**

By Hooke's law, extension is proportional to load. The spring stretches $2\,\text{cm}$ for every $4\,\text{N}$, which is $0.5\,\text{cm}$ per newton.

$$
e = 10\,\text{N} \times 0.5\,\text{cm N}^{-1} = 5\,\text{cm}
$$

**Answer: A**

Option B multiplies the new load by the old extension. Option D turns the ratio upside down.

## Q20 · difficulty 1

**Stem**

Which of the following is a **vector** quantity?

**Options**

- A. Mass
- B. Speed
- C. Energy
- D. Force

**Correct:** D

**Solution**

A vector has both **magnitude and direction**. A force always acts in a particular direction, so it is a vector.

Mass, speed and energy have magnitude only: they are scalars. **Answer: D**

Speed (option B) is a scalar, but **velocity**, which is speed in a given direction, is a vector.
